# ISO 2026

## Purpose

This document is the working calendar for the 2026 edition of Star Almanack.

It uses the ISO 8601 week-numbering year as its basic structure and brings together the astronomical information that will eventually appear in the finished Almanack.

Information will be added incrementally. Each layer should remain understandable on its own while contributing to a single integrated guide to the year and the night sky.

## Zodiac Calendar

Star Almanack divides the tropical zodiac into twelve equal 30° sections of ecliptic longitude.

The zodiac signs are not treated as equivalent to the astronomical constellations bearing similar names.

The calendar date containing a solar ingress is day 1 of the new sign. Each following calendar date increments the sign-day count by 1 until the next ingress.

The ingress times below are calculated by Star Almanack from apparent solar longitude. Published ephemerides are used independently for validation only.

### Validation

The calculated 2026 ingress sequence was compared with independently published tropical-zodiac ingress tables.

The published tables generally report times to the nearest minute. The Star Almanack calculations agree with those published values at that precision throughout the year.

The 4 seasonal boundaries are also independently confirmed by the U.S. Naval Observatory:

- 0° — March equinox

- 90° — June solstice

- 180° — September equinox

- 270° — December solstice

These are calculated by Star Almanack using the same solar-longitude machinery as the other zodiac boundaries.

### Symbol Convention

On the first occurrence of a zodiac sign in the calendar, Star Almanack gives the symbol followed by the name in parentheses.

For example:

♈ (Aries) 1

Subsequent dates use the symbol alone:

♈ 2  

♈ 3  

♈ 4

### Coverage

The December 2025 Capricorn boundary and the 12 ingresses during 2026 provide enough information to assign a zodiac sign and sign-day number to every calendar date from Jan 1 through Dec 31, 2026.

## Objects

Star Almanack records a best-visibility date for 100 selected stars and Messier objects.

Best visibility is the date associated with the annual instant when the object transits the local meridian at 9:00 PM local apparent solar time.

Reader-facing classifications place the declination band before the observing season. Thus an object may be described as **Northern Summer**, **Tropical Winter**, or **Southern Spring**.

Arctic and Antarctic objects are not assigned a season. They retain their best-visibility date because that date identifies when their meridian transit occurs at 9:00 PM, even though their annual visibility behaves differently from that of the other declination bands.
